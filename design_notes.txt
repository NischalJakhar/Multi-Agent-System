Munder Difflin Multi-Agent System — Design Notes
==================================================

Architecture

Built with smolagents: one orchestrator plus three workers, matching the
workflow diagram (workflow_diagram.svg).

The orchestrator is the only agent that talks to the "customer." It has no
tools of its own; it just delegates to the right worker and writes the
final reply. The three workers map onto the three things the project asks
for. inventory_agent checks stock and delivery timing, using
get_stock_level, get_all_inventory, and get_supplier_delivery_date.
quoting_agent prices items through quote_line_item (real catalog price
plus a bulk discount: 5% at 100+, 10% at 500+, 15% at 1000+), using
search_quote_history for rationale. ordering_agent is the only one that
can spend money or record a sale, using get_cash_balance,
generate_financial_report, and create_transaction.

Pricing is computed in code rather than by the LLM, so the number quoted
to a customer is guaranteed to match the number charged. I didn't want to
trust the model to do that arithmetic correctly every time, and that
turned out to matter (see Limitations).

Evaluation

Ran all 20 requests from quote_requests_sample.csv, tracking cash and
inventory before/after each. Cash went from $45,056.45 to $45,386.05,
inventory from $4,940.30 to $4,422.70, with no negative stock anywhere.

Going by the ledger rather than the response text: cash balance changed
on 10 of 20 requests, all backed by a real transaction, and every request
that declines an item states a reason (out of stock, not in catalog,
insufficient quantity for the delivery date, or a restock arriving too
late).

It generally does the right thing: declining unavailable items with the
real stock number cited, not just a generic refusal. Requests 5, 6, and 15
are clean examples — the dollar amount in the response matches the real
cash change exactly.

Limitations

Anything enforceable in code holds up every time: item name resolution,
discount math, the check that blocks a sale from exceeding real stock.
Anything that depends on the model accurately reporting its own actions
does not hold up consistently. From this run's test_results.csv:

Request 1 claims three items were "fulfilled" for $61.75, but cash didn't
move at all. Request 17 has the same problem, claiming $130 in fulfilled
items with no matching transaction. A rule requires the orchestrator to
only say "fulfilled" when ordering_agent returns a transaction ID, which
helps but still depends on the model reporting honestly about whether
that actually happened.

Several other requests state dollar amounts that don't match the real
cash movement even when a transaction did happen — request 14 claims
$705 in fulfilled sales while cash only dropped $31.50, and request 10
claims a $90 sale while cash dropped $278.45. Whatever caused the real
cash movement (probably a stock order) isn't reflected in what the
customer is told.

Request 4 is a different kind of failure: the response isn't addressed to
the customer at all ("The inquiry... has been sent to the customer.
Awaiting their response.") — it reads like an internal status note that
leaked into the customer-facing field instead of an actual answer.

I added a code-level filter that strips cash balance and transaction ID
mentions before a response is returned, since the model doesn't reliably
follow the "never mention this" instruction on its own. It's working (no
raw leaks in this run), though the replacement text ("internal processing
details") occasionally makes a sentence read a little awkwardly rather
than smoothly.

Item matching is exact aside from case/whitespace, so a compound
description like "A4 glossy paper" won't match, even though "A4 paper"
and "Glossy paper" exist separately. It correctly declines rather than
guessing, but that also means it under-fulfills requests a human reader
would probably consider reasonable.

Earlier in development I also hit and fixed more serious issues: a step
timeout that recorded the same sale twice, sales going through with no
stock check (pushing some items negative), and the ordering agent selling
an unrelated item when it couldn't find a match. None of that shows up in
this run; they're why the stock check and single-resolver rule exist.

None of this seems fixable by further tweaking instructions. It comes from
relying on a small model (gpt-4o-mini) to self-report accurately rather
than having every claim independently verified.

Suggestions for improvement

1. Reconcile the response against the ledger before sending it: sum what
   ordering_agent actually returned, compare it to what the orchestrator
   writes, and regenerate if they don't match.

2. Fuzzy item matching instead of exact match, so compound descriptions
   resolve to the closest real SKU instead of failing outright.

3. Make create_transaction idempotent so a retried step can't record the
   same sale twice.

4. Smooth over the sanitization filter so redacted text reads naturally
   instead of dropping in a generic placeholder phrase, and add a check
   that catches responses like request 4's, which aren't addressed to the
   customer at all.

5. Use a stronger model. Nearly every remaining issue here is the model
   not following instructions rather than a logic flaw, so this is
   probably the single highest-leverage change, at the cost of more
   expensive requests.
