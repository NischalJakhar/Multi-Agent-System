Munder Difflin Multi-Agent System — Design Notes
==================================================

Architecture

I built this with smolagents, using 4 agents total: one orchestrator and
three workers. The workflow diagram (workflow_diagram.svg) shows the same
layout, so I'll just explain the reasoning here instead of repeating it.

The orchestrator is the only agent that ever talks to the "customer." It
doesn't have any tools of its own — its whole job is to take the incoming
request, hand pieces of it off to the right worker, and stitch the results
into one reply. I went with this instead of, say, giving the orchestrator
its own tools too, because I wanted a clean separation: the orchestrator
makes decisions and writes the final message, the workers touch the
database.

For the workers, I mapped them straight onto the three things the project
asks for:

- inventory_agent checks stock and delivery timing. It wraps
  get_stock_level, get_all_inventory, and get_supplier_delivery_date.
- quoting_agent prices things. It wraps search_quote_history (mostly for
  rationale/context) and does the actual pricing through quote_line_item,
  which looks up the real catalog price and applies a bulk discount
  (5% at 100+, 10% at 500+, 15% at 1000+).
- ordering_agent is the only one allowed to spend money or record a sale.
  It wraps get_cash_balance, generate_financial_report, and
  create_transaction (for both stock orders and sales).

One decision I made early on: pricing is computed in code, not by the LLM.
quote_line_item and finalize_sale both calculate the price from the real
catalog unit price, so the number quoted to a customer is guaranteed to be
the number actually charged — I didn't want to trust the model to do
arithmetic correctly every time, and as it turned out, that was the right
call (more on that below).

Evaluation

I ran all 20 requests from quote_requests_sample.csv through the system,
tracking cash and inventory before and after each one. The most recent run
started at $45,059.70 cash / $4,940.30 inventory and ended at $45,334.63 /
$4,568.75. Nothing went negative, which wasn't always true in earlier runs
(more on that below too).

Looking at the actual ledger rather than just reading the response text:
cash balance changed on 5 of the 20 requests, and those 5 all have a real
transaction behind them. 15 of the 20 responses give a clear reason why
something couldn't be fulfilled — out of stock, not in the catalog, or
insufficient quantity for the requested delivery date. So the system is
doing what it's supposed to: approving some things, rejecting others, and
explaining why.

What works well: when an item genuinely doesn't exist or is out of stock,
the system says so plainly and usually cites the real number ("only 94
sheets available"). Request 9 is a good example of everything working
end to end — it declines two items with accurate reasons and correctly
sells the third one, and the price it charges matches the cash change in
the ledger exactly.

Where it falls short is consistency, and this took a few rounds of testing
to fully see. I didn't just read the response text and trust it — I kept
cross-checking it against the actual transactions table, and that's where
most of these came from:

Early on, a couple of things were flat-out broken: the agent's code-
execution step had a 30 second timeout that could get hit mid-run, and a
retried step would end up re-selling something it had already sold. Stock
levels weren't checked before a sale went through, so a few items went
into negative stock. And when a customer's wording didn't match a catalog
item exactly (like "A4 glossy paper," which isn't a real SKU — the catalog
has "A4 paper" and "Glossy paper" separately), the ordering agent would
sometimes just sell a completely unrelated item instead of saying it
couldn't find a match. I fixed all three of these — raised the timeout,
added a stock check before any sale, and told the agents explicitly to
decline rather than substitute.

Fixing the substitution problem uncovered a subtler version of it: the
quoting agent and the ordering agent were each independently guessing how
to interpret an ambiguous item name, and sometimes landed on two different
items — so a customer got quoted one price for "Glossy paper" and then
actually charged a different amount for "A4 paper." I fixed this by making
inventory_agent the only agent allowed to resolve an item name, and having
the orchestrator pass that exact resolved name to everyone else instead of
letting each agent reinterpret the customer's wording on its own.

Even after that fix, two things still show up in this latest run:

- A few responses claim an item was "fulfilled" when there's no matching
  transaction in the ledger at all (requests 16 and 17 here). I added a
  rule requiring the orchestrator to only say "fulfilled" if the ordering
  agent actually returned a transaction ID, and that cut this problem
  roughly in half, but it didn't eliminate it — the rule still depends on
  the model reporting honestly, which it doesn't always do.
- The dollar amounts in a few responses don't match what the ledger shows.
  Request 1 talks about $9.75 in sales but cash only moved by $0.50.
  Request 8 lists $540.50 across four items (and mentions internal
  transaction IDs, which it shouldn't) but cash only went up by $128.90.
  Request 13 is the reverse case — cash went up $104 with nothing
  disclosed to the customer at all.

Basically: everything I could enforce in code (item matching, pricing
math, the stock check) holds up every time. Anything that depends on the
model accurately reporting what it did, or using the right date, or not
inventing a reason — that got better with each fix but never became fully
reliable. I don't think that's fixable by tweaking the instructions
further; it's a limitation of using a small model to self-report on its
own actions.

Suggestions for improvement

1. Reconcile the response against the ledger before sending it. The
   cleanest fix for the two issues above would be code, not prompting: add
   the transaction totals ordering_agent actually returned, compare that
   number to whatever the orchestrator writes in the customer message, and
   regenerate the response if they don't match.

2. Better item matching. Right now matching is exact (aside from case and
   whitespace), which is why compound descriptions like "A4 glossy paper"
   trip it up. Some kind of fuzzy or similarity-based matching against the
   catalog would handle these more gracefully than an outright miss.

3. Make transactions idempotent. Give create_transaction an idempotency
   key so a retried step (from a timeout, or the agent just calling a tool
   twice) can never record the same sale twice.

4. Strip internal details before the response goes out. A simple filter
   that removes any mention of cash balance or transaction IDs from the
   final text would catch the leaks that still happen occasionally,
   without relying on the model to remember not to include them.

5. Try a stronger model. I thought about whether this is worth suggesting,
   and I think it genuinely is — pretty much every remaining issue here
   (wrong dates, claiming something happened when it didn't, made-up
   rationale) is the model not following instructions carefully, not a
   flaw in the logic. gpt-4o-mini is cheap and fast, which is why I used
   it, but a stronger model would likely make fewer of these mistakes. It
   costs more per request, so it's a real tradeoff, not a free win — but
   given how much of this report is about instruction-following failures,
   it's probably the single highest-leverage change on this list.
